## 1. Sensory and descriptive language

### 1.1 Taste-led labels raise choice and intake in the field (Turnwald and colleagues)

**Finding.** A preregistered randomised field trial at five US university dining halls (137,842 diner decisions, 185 days, 71 vegetable dishes) found taste-focused labels raised vegetable selection 29% over health-focused labels (95% CI of the log-odds [0.27, 0.66]) and 14% over plain labels (p = .030). Consumption, measured at one school only, rose 39% against health labels with no change in plate waste. The effect was larger where the vegetables were tasty and absent at the school with the least tasty recipes (beta -0.10, ns). In online follow-ups (n = 147 and 67, Mechanical Turk), expected taste experience mediated the effect, and taste-focused wording beat "fancy", vaguely positive and ingredient-list wording matched for length. Source: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6843749 (Psychological Science 2019).
**Evidence kind.** Field RCT by the original authors, preregistered. No independent replication found. The authors list limits: consumption at one site, no individual-level tracking, mechanism tested only online.
**Earlier 2017 JAMA Internal Medicine letter** (single dining hall, one academic quarter): indulgent labels beat plain by 25% in selection; individual intake not measured. Numbers from a news summary of the paper, https://news.stanford.edu/2017/06/12/decadent-sounding-labeling-may-lead-people-eat-vegetables/ (unverified, not opened; secondary).
**Design implication (inference).** Words work through expected taste, and only when the underlying food is genuinely good. Copy should promise taste experiences (the first bite, the texture) rather than ornament. This is evidence about people choosing among foods, not about producing craving in someone who rejects the dish.

### 1.2 Consumption-simulation wording (Papies and colleagues)

**Finding.** Descriptions built from sensory, eating-context and hedonic ("rewarding") features raised the appeal and order intention of novel plant-based dishes versus control descriptions: three online studies, N = 653, 246 and 1,204, omnivores and vegans in the rating stages. Source: https://strathprints.strath.ac.uk/93602/1/Farrar-etal-FQP-2024-Increasing-the-appeal-of-plant-based-foods-through-describing-the-consumption.pdf (Food Quality and Preference 2024; I read the abstract and method headings, not every result table; no effect size recorded).
The same paper says earlier work (Papies et al. 2020) found such descriptions raised perceived attractiveness of plant-based dishes, and that frequent meat eaters rated plant-based dishes less attractive. A search summary says the earlier effect was on attractiveness but weaker on desire and purchase intention (unverified, not opened). Theory and a 2013 feature-listing result (eating-related features were about 53% of features listed for tempting foods versus about 26% for neutral foods) come from a search summary of https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3829564 (page would not open; unverified).
**Evidence kind.** Online experiments, one research group, written descriptions of dishes with no tasting. Closest available analogue to the project's "dislikers" problem, because the target audience held a prior belief that the food is less tasty.
**Design implication (inference).** Write the tasting copy as a short sequence of sensory, setting and pleasure phrases ("steam on your face, rice that falls apart, hot, in a cold room"). Test the lines on people who dislike biryani, since the only analogous result is for a different disliked food (plant-based dishes) and a different audience.

### 1.3 Multisensory ad copy (Elder and Krishna 2010)

**Finding.** Ads that cued several senses produced higher perceived taste than ads about taste alone, mediated by an excess of positive over negative sensory thoughts; the effect shrank under cognitive load. Three experiments (gum first, then crisps and popcorn). Source for abstract-level facts: https://scholarsarchive.byu.edu/facpub/8440 and https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:oup:jconrs:v:36:y:2010:i:5:p:748-756 (abstract page opened; full text paywalled; sample sizes and effect sizes not checked).
**Evidence kind.** Single paper, three experiments; sample sizes not read. No replication found.
**Design implication (inference).** Cue several senses in each beat, but keep the reading load low. Dense paragraphs of sensory adjectives may backfire if attention is split.

### 1.4 Descriptive menu labels and the Wansink problem

**Finding.** The classic claim that descriptive menu labels raise sales is Wansink, Painter and van Ittersum 2001 (Cornell Hotel and Restaurant Administration Quarterly). My search found no retraction notice for that paper, but found that a related Wansink paper, "Attractive names sustain increased vegetable intake in schools" (Preventive Medicine 2012), is marked RETRACTED, and other Wansink papers were retracted (https://retractionwatch.com/category/by-journal/j-sensory-studies, https://www.cornellsun.com/article/2018/10/food-science-researcher-brian-wansink-faces-two-more-retractions; both unverified, search summaries only). I could not confirm the status of the 2001 paper.
**Evidence kind.** Replication and integrity concerns around the author. Treat as unreliable until checked at the publisher page (DOI 10.1177/0010880401426008, unverified).
**Design implication (inference).** Do not cite Wansink for any claim in the project. Lean on Turnwald 2019 and Papies instead, which are independent of that lab.

### 1.5 Which word types work (texture, temperature, situation, sound)

**Finding.** Gap. The Farrar data-driven procedure rated "sensory, context and hedonic" features but I did not extract which individual features (texture, heat, setting) scored highest (abstract only at hand). I found no study isolating sound words, or temperature words, as the active ingredient.
**Design implication (inference).** Treat texture, heat, setting and sound words as a hypothesis to test, not a result.

## 2. Food cue reactivity, and sound

### 2.1 Cue reactivity and craving predict eating (Boswell and Kober 2016)

**Finding.** Meta-analysis: 69 statistics from 45 reports, 3,292 participants; overall r = 0.33 (about 11% of variance) between cue reactivity or craving and later eating or weight outcomes. Visual cues (pictures, videos) had an effect size similar to real food and larger than olfactory cues. No significant moderation by BMI, age or dietary restraint. Source: https://canlab.yale.edu/sites/default/files/boswel-kober_2016_obesityreviews.pdf (Obesity Reviews 17:159-177; abstract and introduction read).
**Important limit.** The outcome is whether reactivity predicts eating; the paper does not measure how strongly pictures raise craving in the first place, and it says nothing about people who dislike the food. Much of it is lab work in people who are in the target population for the cue.
**Design implication (inference).** Pictures and videos are a legitimate craving route, but this result does not show they work on a dislike.

### 2.2 "Visual hunger" (Spence and colleagues)

**Finding.** Review in Brain and Cognition (Oxford record dates it 2015; volume 2016) argues that food images strongly affect neural, physiological and psychological responses and visual attention, especially in hungry people, and proposes "visual hunger". The abstract frames the link to real eating as a question to investigate, not established. Source: https://ora.ox.ac.uk/objects/uuid:2cfc871d-1321-4d3a-a637-04fd04996d69 (abstract only; not clear whether it is systematic).
**Design implication (inference).** Hunger state is the moderator; the experience cannot assume it. Offer a "time of day" cue (evening, rain) rather than count on real hunger.

### 2.3 Eating sounds, ASMR, mukbang

**Finding (mixed).**
- A Nottingham Trent study (search summary) reports that ASMR mukbang, but not standard mukbang, significantly raised appetite, desire to eat and expected consumption of the foods shown, with mood as a mediator. I could not open the record; sample and journal unverified. https://irep.ntu.ac.uk/48713/1/1749405_Wan.pdf redirected, so it is cited only as a search hit.
- An NTU Singapore experiment found the opposite direction after repeated viewing: 30 views of a first-person video of someone eating candy led to about a third less candy eaten than a control video (study 1 n = 42, study 2 n = 114). https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-023-35669-5 (Scientific Reports 2023; numbers from a news summary at https://www.eurekalert.org/news-releases/999774; unverified).
- A claim that watching others eat reduces craving, and another that it raises later eating in restrained eaters, came from a secondary article and are unverified (https://www.scienceabc.com/social-science/what-is-the-psychology-of-mukbang-watching.html).
**Cooking sounds (sizzle).** I found no study that tests cooking sounds on craving or salivation. Spence-group work covers crunch and perceived freshness, and a 180-item kitchen-sound norming set (search summaries; https://www.neuroscience.ox.ac.uk/publications/60686, unverified). The opening 02 doc already cites the crunch finding.
**Salivation.** No salivation result for food words, pictures or sound was found.
**Design implication (inference).** Sound is plausible but unproven for craving. Use it as the strongest bet, and keep it short and varied (see section 5) because repeated eating audio may satiate rather than tempt.

## 3. Anticipation and delay

**Finding.** Nowlis, Mandel and McCabe 2004 (Journal of Consumer Research 31:502-510): in three studies, an imposed delay between choice and consumption raised enjoyment of a pleasurable product when it was really consumed, but lowered enjoyment when consumption was only imagined; vividness of the awaited product moderated. Source (abstract page opened; no sample sizes or effect sizes read): https://profiles.wustl.edu/en/publications/the-effect-of-a-delay-between-choice-and-consumption-on-consumpti/
**Dopamine caveat.** Not researched this session. The gap to flag: popular "dopamine peaks in anticipation" claims are not backed by anything I have opened, so the design should not cite dopamine.
**Design implication (inference).** The dum wait helps the later lid-lift only if the viewer is waiting for something they really expect to get. A pure on-screen "imagined" wait could reduce enjoyment according to this paper, so make the viewer see and hear the pot while waiting.

## 4. Effort and self-preparation

**Findings.**
- IKEA effect: Norton, Mochon and Ariely 2012 (Journal of Consumer Psychology 22:453-460). Labour raised valuation of what people built, but only when they completed it. https://scholars.duke.edu/publication/861385 (search summary; unverified). A "nearly five times" origami figure came from a blog and is not used.
- Self-prepared food: Troye and Supphellen 2012 (Journal of Marketing), three experiments with a dinner kit; the swapped-in chef-made casserole was rated better by those who believed they made it. https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:spr:sprchp:978-3-8349-6976-7_8 is a loosely related record (search summary; unverified).
- Virtual or imagined effort: not researched; no evidence found that it transfers.
**Design implication (inference).** Let the player finish each step (a completed step is what the effect needs) and avoid failure states. Do not assume a tap-to-stir counts as "effort".

## 5. Satiation risk, and whether variety offsets it

### 5.1 Imagined consumption reduces later eating (Morewedge, Huh, Vosgerau 2010)

**Finding.** Five experiments (Science 330:1530-1533): repeated imagined eating of a food (30 repetitions in the original) reduced later actual consumption of that food, through habituation; the effect was in wanting, not liking. https://stafforini.com/works/morewedge-2010-thought-food-imagined/ (search summary; unverified). 
**Replications.** Mixed. Missbach and colleagues replicated it with gummy bears (n = 95; 24.6 g eaten versus 32.9 g control) and walnuts (n = 82 women; effect only in non-depleted participants, interaction p = 0.08), with the effect at 18 repetitions and none extra at 36. Liking did not change. https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2014.01391/full (opened). Two small Czech student replications (n = 77 and one other) found no effect (https://e-psycholog.eu/pdf/Michele_etal.pdf, search summary; unverified).
**Design implication (inference).** The threshold is in the order of tens of repetitions, far above what a 4.5-minute piece depicts. The risk is real but small at this scale. Still, do not loop identical eating beats.

### 5.2 Viewing many similar food pictures

**Finding.** Larson, Redden and Elder 2014 (Journal of Consumer Psychology 24:188-194): in two studies, repeatedly rating or choosing among pictures of similar foods decreased later enjoyment of similar foods; they attribute it to spontaneous simulation of taste. https://experts.umn.edu/en/publications/satiation-from-sensory-simulation-evaluating-foods-decreases-enjo/ (abstract page opened; no sample sizes given there). The abstract says the effect is strongest for foods sharing a similar taste characteristic, and that mediation evidence shows simulation of taste alone is enough to produce satiation.
**Design implication (inference).** Keep the number of similar close-ups of the same food low; vary the category (rice, onion, steam, spice, meat) across beats.

### 5.3 Does variety offset it?

**Finding.** Sensory-specific satiety is the premise of 5.2 (enjoyment falls for the sensory properties just experienced). I found no study that tests whether varied cues restore desire in the picture or imagery case. The Larson abstract (opened) says the satiation effect is strongest for foods sharing a similar taste, which implies dissimilar foods are less affected, but that is an inference from the abstract, not a tested result.
**Design implication (inference).** Variety across sensory category is probably protective; this is untested for a short experience.

## 6. Ease of mental simulation

**Finding.** Elder and Krishna 2012 (Journal of Consumer Research 38:988-1003): four studies; orienting a product (a spoon or handle) toward the viewer's dominant hand raised purchase intention, via embodied mental simulation; the effect reversed for negatively valenced products; it needed an instrument such as a spoon. https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:oup:jconrs:doi:10.1086/661531 (abstract via search; unverified). Motion in food images and first-person framing: not researched.
**Design implication (inference).** Point the spoon toward the viewer's free hand; the reversal for negative products means a disliker may be pushed further away if the eating cue is shown too forcefully.

## 7. Disliked or unfamiliar foods

**Findings.**
- Food neophobia scale: Pliner and Hobden 1992 (Appetite 19:105-120), a 10-item scale that predicted lab food choices. https://scholar.uwindsor.ca/psychologypub/39 (search summary; unverified). Cross-cultural: a Japanese adaptation notes unstable factor structure by cultural background, and a southern African scale (South Africa, Lesotho, Botswana) found two factors, approach and avoidance, needing confirmation elsewhere (search summaries; unverified). No cross-country comparison of neophobia levels was found.
- Mere exposure: Pliner 1982 and a 2018 meta-analysis (Appleton and colleagues; 43 articles, 117 comparisons) found repeated tasting increases liking of vegetables. Mostly children; taste exposure, not viewing or reading (search summaries; unverified).
- Other people's faces: Barthomeuf and colleagues 2009 (44 adults, photographs): a disgusted eater lowered desire to eat the foods, and a pleased eater raised desire for disliked foods. https://hal.inrae.fr/hal-02658006 (search result; unverified). Rousset and colleagues 2008: a neutral or happy eater raised desire for meat products (unverified).
**What is not known.** Whether words, sound or animated images change wanting for biryani in someone who dislikes it; whether the effects last; whether results hold outside US, UK and European samples.
**Design implication (inference).** A visible, happy eater is the best supported cue for disliked foods, so show a character's pleasure at the table. Read "show a face, not only the dish" as a hypothesis.

## What this cannot tell us

- Whether any of this works on a viewer who dislikes the dish. No source directly tests it; the nearest analogues are plant-based dishes (omnivores), vegetables, and photographs with eaters.
- Whether sizzling or cooking sound raises craving or salivation. No study found.
- Whether reading or viewing satiation matters at 4.5 minutes; the lab thresholds are tens of repetitions.
- Almost all sources are Western, mostly US university or online samples; effects in South Asian audiences are unknown.
- Many facts above are from search-result summaries and are flagged "(unverified)"; open the DOIs before quoting any number in the project.
- Not researched: dopamine claims, virtual effort, motion in food images.
